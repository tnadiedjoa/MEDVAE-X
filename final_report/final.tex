\section{Introduction}

Deep learning on medical images is constrained by the high resolution required to preserve diagnostic detail, which inflates the computational and memory cost of every model. Large-scale variational autoencoders (VAE) offer a way out by compressing images into compact latent representations. We focus on MedVAE~\cite{varma2025medvaeefficientautomatedinterpretation}, a representative example: trained on more than one million medical images, it learns a generic, reusable compression of medical images --- in the variant we use, reducing a $512\times512$ image by a factor of $4$ per side (sixteen times fewer values) into a single-channel $128\times128$ latent --- while seeking to preserve clinically relevant structures, and is itself built in two stages: a first stage dedicated to faithful reconstruction, and a second stage meant to enrich the latent space with semantic knowledge. Such generalist models raise a natural question, which we take as our starting point: to what extent does a generic medical compressor remain trustworthy and useful once confronted with a specific, demanding clinical domain?

We study this question on coronary angiography, using the public ARCADE dataset~\cite{popov2024arcade}, which provides $1\,500$ annotated X-ray angiographies ($512\times512$, grayscale; $1\,000$ training, $200$ validation and $300$ test images) together with COCO segmentation annotations of the coronary tree. We deliberately chose an unfavourable setting for a generic compressor: the structures of interest are thin, tubular, and low-contrast, the acquisitions suffer from quantum noise, compression, and motion artefacts, and the relevant signal occupies only a tiny fraction of the image. This domain therefore stresses every property we would expect from a medical foundation model: robustness to degraded inputs, a semantically structured latent space, and the preservation of fine anatomical information.

Starting from MedVAE and ARCADE, we identify and investigate four complementary axes. \textbf{(i) Robustness to input degradations} (Section~\ref{sec:medvae-robustness}): we measure whether MedVAE brings a degraded input (Poisson noise, JPEG compression, blur) closer to the clean image, showing that it reduces Poisson noise, though much less than a simple $5\times5$ filter, and restores neither JPEG artefacts nor blur. \textbf{(ii) Image-quality inductive bias} (Section~\ref{sec:theophile}): we ask whether explicitly conditioning the autoencoder on an estimated quality score, injected through FiLM modulation, lets it adapt to the degradation level actually present at its input. \textbf{(iii) Semantic structuring of the latent space} (Section~\ref{sec:jepa}): we replace MedVAE's stage-2 BioMedCLIP alignment with a self-supervised JEPA objective trained directly on coronary angiographies, removing any dependency on an external model. \textbf{(iv) Latent representation for segmentation} (Section~\ref{sec:segmentation}): we test whether the compressed representation preserves enough anatomical information to support fine multi-class coronary segmentation, and at which point of the pipeline the compression should be integrated.

We each took on one of these axes --- Elias Corlou the robustness analysis, Théophile Nadiedjoa the image-quality conditioning, Yanic Rothlingshofer the JEPA adaptation, and Théo Palagi the segmentation study --- and we present our four contributions in the corresponding sections before bringing them together in a common discussion of perspectives and a shared conclusion.

\section{Robustness of MedVAE to Input Degradations}
\label{sec:medvae-robustness}

MedVAE was evaluated, in its original paper~\cite{varma2025medvaeefficientautomatedinterpretation}, on
clean inputs --- by PSNR and MS-SSIM as well as by expert reader
studies. One question remains open for real-world deployment:
\emph{what does MedVAE do with an input image that is not clean, but
degraded?} In coronary angiography, this question is far from
theoretical: low-dose acquisition induces significant quantum noise,
and clinical images undergo compression, motion blur, and various
artefacts~\cite{cesarelli2013xray}.

We evaluate this on the ARCADE dataset~\cite{popov2024arcade}. The
framework is entirely \emph{full-reference}: for each image we
simultaneously have the clean image, a controlled degraded version, and
the reconstruction produced by MedVAE from the degraded input. The
research question is: \textbf{does passing a degraded image through
MedVAE bring it closer to, or further from, the clean image?} We show
that the answer depends on the \emph{nature} of the degradation: MedVAE
reduces Poisson noise, though much less than a simple $5\times5$ filter,
and restores neither JPEG artefacts nor blur.

\emph{Note on this revised version.} An earlier version of this study
measured the fidelity of the reconstruction to the \emph{degraded}
input, with a per-image min-max normalisation that placed the clean and
degraded images on different intensity scales. Both choices biased the
conclusions (Section~\ref{sec:cadre}); the protocol and all results
below were redone accordingly.

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:cadre}

\subsubsection{Why No-Reference Metrics Were Discarded}
\label{sec:no-ref}

A first, natural approach consists of quantifying the quality of the
input image by a \emph{no-reference} metric, then correlating it with
reconstruction quality. We explored this route with
ARNIQA~\cite{agnolucci2024arniqa}, as well as with a custom score
aggregating sharpness indices. Two limitations disqualified it. On one
hand, these metrics are calibrated on natural photographs and transfer
poorly to X-ray images: on the raw images of the dataset, the
correlation between the no-reference score and the reconstruction PSNR
is nearly zero. On the other hand, under controlled degradation, these
scores are dominated by blur and do not provide a monotone quality
axis~\cite{agnolucci2024arniqa}. Our protocol being full-reference, we
instead \emph{measure} input quality by comparison to the clean image.

\subsubsection{Measuring Restoration, Not Fidelity to the Input}
\label{sec:measures}

Three PSNR values can be computed for each degraded image:
\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0.2em
\item $\mathrm{PSNR}(\text{clean}, \text{degraded})$: how far the input
      is from the clean image (degradation amplitude);
\item $\mathrm{PSNR}(\text{clean}, \text{reconstructed})$: how far
      MedVAE's output is from the clean image;
\item $\mathrm{PSNR}(\text{degraded}, \text{reconstructed})$: how
      faithfully MedVAE reproduces the input it was given.
\end{itemize}
The third quantity, used in the earlier version of this study, does not
measure robustness: a blurred image, stripped of its high frequencies,
is simply easier to reproduce, so this fidelity rises with blur even if
the output moves away from the clean image. The quantity that answers
our question is the restoration gain
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \mathrm{PSNR}(\text{clean}, \text{reconstructed}) - \mathrm{PSNR}(\text{clean}, \text{degraded}),
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
positive when MedVAE's output is closer to the clean image than its
input was. All images are compared on the same fixed intensity scale
($[0,1]$, pixel value$/255$).

\subsubsection{Vessel-Masked PSNR}
\label{sec:masked-psnr}

A PSNR computed over the full image mostly reflects the fidelity of the
background, which occupies the vast majority of the surface. We
therefore also restrict the computation to vessel pixels, obtained by
rasterising ARCADE's polygonal annotations (COCO format) into a binary
mask, which defines a masked PSNR denoted $\mathrm{mPSNR}$. Every
quantity above is computed both on the full image and in this masked
version (details in Appendix~\ref{app:mpsnr}).

\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:methode}

\subsubsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocole}

We use the \texttt{medvae\_4\_1\_2d} autoencoder (X-ray modality) on
$100$ images of ARCADE's official segmentation validation split
(\texttt{seg\_val}). Each image, scaled to $[-1,1]$ as MedVAE expects,
is encoded and decoded \emph{deterministically} (mean of the
approximate posterior, instead of a random sample). For each
degradation type, $10$ levels of increasing severity are applied, the
noise being drawn with a generator seeded per image and per level; the
clean image is also reconstructed as a reference. Metrics are averaged
over images, with $95\%$ confidence intervals obtained by bootstrap
over images. The code is in \texttt{medvae\_eval/robustness/}.

\subsection{Degradation Types}
\label{sec:degradations}

Three degradations are studied, each swept independently, over the same
ranges as in the earlier version of the study:

\textbf{Poisson noise.} More realistic than Gaussian noise for X-rays,
where quantum noise (\emph{shot noise}) dominates at low
dose~\cite{cesarelli2013xray}. Intensity is controlled by a decreasing
scale factor (from $1$ to $0.05$): as the scale decreases, fewer
photons are simulated and the noise becomes more pronounced.

\textbf{JPEG compression.} Quality factor decreasing from $95$ to $5$,
successively introducing quantisation of DCT coefficients and then
$8\times8$ block artefacts.

\textbf{Gaussian blur.} Kernel size increasing from $1$ (clean image)
to $31$ pixels.

\subsection{Results and Interpretation}
\label{sec:resultats}

MedVAE reconstructs these clean images at $33.1$~dB, both on the full
image and on vessels ($34.8$~dB on the 300 test images used in
Section~\ref{sec:jepa}): this intrinsic reconstruction error sets a
ceiling that no restoration can exceed. As a reference, the same noisy
images were also passed through two simple filters, a $5\times5$
Gaussian filter and a $5\times5$ median filter. Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep-all} shows the three
PSNR curves and the restoration gain $\Delta$ on vessel pixels, and
Table~\ref{tab:restoration} summarises $\Delta$ over the sweep.

\subsubsection{Poisson Noise: Reduced, Less Than by a Simple Filter}
\label{sec:poisson}

Poisson noise adds decorrelated high frequencies, precisely what the
latent bottleneck cannot represent, so MedVAE suppresses
it~\cite{vincent2008denoising}. The reconstruction is closer to the
clean image than the noisy input for $99$ to $100\%$ of the images at
every level, and the gain grows with the noise, from $+1.2$~dB at the
weakest level to $+6.6$~dB ($+6.8$~dB on vessels) at the strongest. This
noise reduction is, however, modest: a $5\times5$ Gaussian filter gains
$+6.3$ to $+10.3$~dB on the same images and a $5\times5$ median filter
$+7.2$ to $+10.0$~dB, 4 to 5~dB more than MedVAE at every level. MedVAE's
gain is bounded by its own reconstruction error: even for mild noise,
the output ($28$~dB) stays well below the reconstruction of the clean
image ($33$~dB).

\subsubsection{JPEG Compression: No Restoration}
\label{sec:jpeg}

JPEG artefacts are small in PSNR terms (the input is at $44.7$~dB of
the clean image at quality $95$), far below MedVAE's own reconstruction
error. Passing the image through MedVAE therefore always moves it away
from the clean image, by $11.7$~dB at quality $95$ and $0.5$~dB at
quality $5$. The earlier observation of a ``two-regime'' response
concerned the fidelity to the degraded input and does not translate
into any restoration.

\subsubsection{Gaussian Blur: No Restoration}
\label{sec:flou}

Blur removes high frequencies that the latent cannot recover. The
reconstruction simply follows the blurred input: $\Delta$ is negative
for mild blur (the reconstruction error adds to the blur) and close to
zero for strong blur on the full image, while it remains slightly
negative on vessels. The earlier finding that ``blurred inputs
reconstruct better'' is visible in the orange curve of
Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep-all} (fidelity to the blurred input rising from
$33.1$ to $47.7$~dB on vessels): it measures
how easy a blurred image is to reproduce, not how well MedVAE recovers
the clean one, which is the opposite trend (blue curve).

\subsection{Discussion and Perspectives}
\label{sec:discussion}

MedVAE acts as a low-pass autoencoder: it attenuates stochastic high
frequencies (Poisson noise), though less than a simple $5\times5$
filter, it cannot recover information removed by blur, and its own
reconstruction error exceeds the mild artefacts of JPEG compression. Beyond the degradation itself, the
$33$~dB reconstruction ceiling is the main limit of any restoration
through MedVAE on this out-of-distribution modality.

These characteristics call for several clinical evaluation perspectives.
First, it will be necessary to quantify the impact of this low-pass
dynamic on stenosis detection, the central task of the ARCADE dataset,
as these pathologies constitute fine structures particularly sensitive
to smoothing; measuring segmentation quality on degraded images
shows that MedVAE's denoising does not make segmentation more robust
(Section~\ref{sec:segmentation}).
Furthermore, evaluating robustness to alterations of the grey-level
dynamic range (gamma variations, underexposure) is a natural next step.
Finally, the protocol would benefit from integrating motion blur,
induced by cardiac and respiratory dynamics, whose spectral signature is
far more complex than the isotropic blur modelled here.

\section{Image-quality inductive bias for MedVAE}
\label{sec:theophile}

\subsection{Motivation}

The previous section showed that MedVAE removes Poisson noise but
restores neither JPEG artefacts nor blur, and that it has no explicit way
of knowing \emph{how much} the image it is given is degraded: it applies
the same transformation to a clean or a heavily noisy angiography. Yet this
information is available \emph{a priori} and can be estimated by
no-reference quality metrics (NR-IQA). We hypothesise that explicitly
conditioning the autoencoder on a quality score $c \in [0,1]$ improves its
reconstruction, by letting it adapt its behaviour to the degradation level
actually present at the input. Three questions follow: how to compute $c$
objectively and reproducibly in the absence of human annotation on
ARCADE; how to inject it into MedVAE without retraining the model from
scratch; and whether any observed gain is attributable to the informative
content of $c$ or to a mere additional-capacity effect. This last
question is far from trivial: any conditioning mechanism adds parameters
and changes the training dynamics, so an apparent gain could simply
reflect increased capacity rather than a genuine exploitation of the
quality signal, hence the need for a dedicated ablation
(section~\ref{sec:ablation}). For the first question, three independent
methods for computing $c$ (approaches A, B, C) are compared; the
conditioning is studied in depth with approach C (three training seeds)
and checked with A and B, which makes it possible to distinguish an
effect specific to a given method of computing $c$ from a more general
effect of the FiLM conditioning itself.

\subsection{Construction of the quality score}
\label{sec:score}

On the segmentation subset of ARCADE (1000 train / 200 validation images,
$512\times512$, grayscale), each image is split into a $4\times4$ grid of
patches, a local granularity motivated by the spatially non-uniform
nature of artefacts in cardiac imaging (motion, noise)
\cite{sekiguchi2025patchdsa}, normalised by \emph{Mean Subtracted Contrast
Normalization} ($\hat{I}=(I-\mu)/(\sigma+C)$, $7\times7$ window), then
characterised by 8 metrics per patch: Laplacian variance, RMS contrast,
Shannon entropy, and the Tenengrad gradient broken down into 4
directions, a directional analysis suited to the tubular structure of
vessels \cite{wang2024noreference} (which allows detection of anisotropic
cardiac motion blur). These per-patch metrics are then aggregated over
the patches of each image (mean, minimum and similar statistics). A pre-trained ResNet-18 (frozen weights) extracts in parallel a
512-dimensional feature vector, used only by the learned approach: the
two branches of this feature pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:featpipe}) never
converge directly, they simply feed different score-computation methods,
described below.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{figures/theophile/pipeline_A_features.pdf}
  \caption{Construction of quality features: per-patch IQA metrics and
  ResNet-18 features.}
  \label{fig:featpipe}
\end{figure}

These features are then merged into a single score $c$, with $1$
corresponding to a clinically usable image, using three independent
methods (Figure~\ref{fig:scorepipe}), all fitted exclusively on the TRAIN
subset. Approach B applies a PCA (after standardisation) on 6 selected
features (four of the aggregated metrics --- mean Laplacian variance,
minimum RMS contrast, inverse entropy and mean Tenengrad --- plus a
spatial-homogeneity and a directional-balance feature); the first axis explains 56.1\,\% of the variance, and its sign
is corrected by correlation with Tenengrad ($r=0.952$). Approach A
linearly weights the same 6 features by the absolute normalised loadings
of this same PC1. Approach C, lacking human annotation on ARCADE, learns
a score on a \emph{synthetic proxy}, an indirect supervision strategy
already used to calibrate NR-IQA scores on biomedical images without
perceptual ground truth \cite{asri2026deeplearning}: each TRAIN image is
artificially degraded (noise, blur, JPEG) with a target
$y=1-\text{known severity}$, and a lightweight MLP
($512{\to}128{\to}32{\to}1$) is trained by MSE on the ResNet-18 features of
this synthetic set (validation MSE $1.58\times10^{-3}$), then applied to
the real images.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/theophile/pipeline_B_score.pdf}
  \caption{The three approaches for computing the quality score $c$.}
  \label{fig:scorepipe}
\end{figure*}

A and B, built on the same metrics, are strongly correlated ($r=0.884$);
C, in contrast, is nearly independent of both
($r(A,C)=0.003$, $r(B,C)=0.015$, Table~\ref{tab:corr}) and its
distribution is much more tightly concentrated near 1 (mean 0.890) than A
(0.448) or B (0.483): the MLP learned on deep features captures a notion
of quality that differs from a combination of classical metrics. The
active score is finally discretised into three classes
\emph{bad}/\emph{medium}/\emph{good} by tertiles (TRAIN). Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}
illustrates this progression on real images sorted by increasing score:
the least sharp and noisiest angiographies (left) progressively give way
to contrasted acquisitions where the vascular tree is clearly defined
(right), which qualitatively confirms that the score captures a notion of
image quality consistent with a clinical reading.

\subsection{FiLM conditioning}
\label{sec:film}

To condition MedVAE \cite{varma2025medvaeefficientautomatedinterpretation} on $c$ without modifying its
input topology, we use \emph{Feature-wise Linear Modulation}
(FiLM \cite{perez2018film}) rather than channel concatenation. We work
with the \texttt{vae\_4x\_4c\_2D} variant of MedVAE
(\texttt{AutoencoderKL} \cite{kingma2014vae}, factor $4$ per side, $4$
latent channels, 55.3M parameters). A shared MLP encodes $c$ into a
128-dimensional embedding, and 19 linear heads (one per
\texttt{ResnetBlock} of the encoder, bottleneck, and decoder) produce
affine parameters applied through a hook on each block,
$h \leftarrow (1+\gamma_i(c)) \cdot h + \beta_i(c)$. The heads are
initialised at zero, so the conditioned model is identical to the
original model before any training: any difference observed afterwards
stems from what the network has learned, not from a random perturbation
of the pre-trained weights. FiLM also leaves the encoder's topology
intact and concentrates the conditioning on clearly identifiable
parameters. This mechanism adds 1.70M parameters (3.1\,\%).

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:film-protocol}

\emph{Note on this revised version.} An earlier version of this study
trained and evaluated all models at $64\times64$, a resolution at which
the pre-trained MedVAE only reaches $20.7$~dB on our test images (against
$34.8$~dB at $256\times256$) and vessels shrink to about one pixel. Its
loss added the KL divergence \emph{summed} over the latent to a
\emph{per-pixel mean} squared error, so that the KL term dominated the
objective (at $256\times256$ it outweighs the reconstruction error by a
factor of about $700$ and makes the validation error rise during
training); the conditioned model and the baseline followed different
training schedules; and every comparison relied on a single training
run per model. The protocol and all results below were redone
accordingly.

\textbf{Data.} The 1000 images of the ARCADE training split are divided
once and for all into 900 training and 100 validation images; the 200
images of the official validation split serve as the \emph{test set},
never seen during training or model selection. Images are resized to
$256\times256$.

\textbf{Training.} The baseline (MedVAE without conditioning) and the
FiLM model are trained in exactly the same way: all weights, 3000 steps,
AdamW (learning rate $10^{-5}$, cosine decay to $10^{-6}$), batch size
4, loss $=$ MSE $+\,10^{-6}\,\mathrm{KL}/N_{\text{pixels}}$ (the KL is
normalised by the number of pixels, following the MedVAE and
latent-diffusion convention). The checkpoint with the lowest validation
reconstruction error is kept. Each model is trained with 3 seeds.

\textbf{Evaluation.} On the test set, we compute the PSNR, the PSNR
restricted to vessel pixels (mPSNR, Section~\ref{sec:masked-psnr}), SSIM
and HaarPSI, the latter being recommended for medical
images~\cite{breger2024adequacy}. To test whether the FiLM model uses
the \emph{content} of $c$, each FiLM model is evaluated three times
(Figure~\ref{fig:film-protocol}): with the true score of each image, with
the score of another test image (a fixed random permutation of the
scores), and with a constant score (the training mean). If the network
exploits the quality information, the true score must beat both
controls. Unlike an ablation that trains a second model with a constant
score, these controls are computed on the \emph{same} trained model, so
their differences are free of the variability between training runs.

three seeds but an order of magnitude below any perceptible difference.
The earlier conclusion that the conditioned model reconstructs markedly
worse than the baseline does not hold once both models are trained in
the same way.

\subsection{Is the quality signal used?}
\label{sec:ablation}

The controls answer this question directly. With score C, replacing the
true score of each image by that of another image changes the PSNR by $+0.002$~dB
($95\,\%$ CI $[-0.007, 0.010]$), and a constant score does even slightly
\emph{better} than the true one ($-0.013$~dB, $[-0.021, -0.006]$); the
same holds for the vessel PSNR, SSIM and HaarPSI. The FiLM parameters
did move during training, and they do depend on $c$ (the modulation
$\gamma$ differs by up to $0.05$ between $c=0$ and $c=1$), but this
dependence does not help reconstruction: the small gain over the
baseline comes from the added per-channel affine parameters, not from
the quality information.

Three additional runs (seed 42) check that this conclusion does not
depend on the choice of score or on the learning rate of the FiLM layers
(Table~\ref{tab:film-checks}). With a learning rate 100 times higher for
the FiLM layers, the conditioned model gains $0.24$~dB over the baseline,
but a constant score does exactly as well as the true one: the FiLM
layers act as additional parameters, not as a channel for the quality
information. With scores A and B, the true score does beat both controls,
with confidence intervals excluding zero: the network exploits these
scores slightly. The effect remains tiny, at most $0.04$~dB on the image
and $0.01$~dB on vessels.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion-theophile}

Measured with identical training, an independent test set and controls
computed on the same model, conditioning MedVAE on an image-quality score
brings no useful gain in reconstruction: the learned score C is not used
at all, and scores A and B are used only marginally. This revises both
conclusions of the earlier version of this study (a conditioned model
markedly worse than the baseline, and score C as the only one genuinely
exploited): both were obtained under the conditions listed in
Section~\ref{sec:film-protocol}, and neither survives the revised
protocol.

A simple reason limits what the score can bring. All three scores are
computed \emph{from the image itself} (quality metrics for A and B,
ResNet-18 features for C): for an autoencoder whose target is its own
input, $c$ contains no information that the encoder does not already
see. At most it summarises a global property of the image (overall
sharpness or contrast) that a purely convolutional network only
perceives locally, which may explain the marginal use of scores A and B.
A quality signal could only bring new information in a setting where it
is not a function of the input, for instance a restoration task
(degraded input, clean target) conditioned on acquisition parameters
known independently of the image, such as the dose. Two further limitations remain: a single
training budget was explored (and scores A and B were tested with one
seed), and the absence of human quality
annotation on ARCADE means that the three scores could only be validated
indirectly (mutual consistency, correlation with reference metrics).

\section{JEPA Adaptation of MedVAE Stage 2}
\label{sec:jepa}

As introduced above, MedVAE~\cite{varma2025medvaeefficientautomatedinterpretation} is trained in two stages, the second of which enriches the latent space with semantic knowledge --- the stage we revisit here. Medical imaging raises specific challenges --- monochrome images, scarce annotations, heterogeneous modalities --- that make learning robust representations particularly difficult.

\subsection{Motivations}

In its original formulation, MedVAE stage 2 aligns the latent space with BioMedCLIP representations, a foundation model pre-trained on paired medical image--text corpora. In MedVAE, this stage freezes the encoder and decoder and trains only light projection layers on top of the latent, with an $L_2$ loss between their output and the BioMedCLIP image embedding of the same image; neither BioMedCLIP nor text is needed at inference. This approach has two limitations. First, training depends on an external model. Second, and more fundamentally, the alignment inherits BioMedCLIP's biases and coverage gaps: coronary angiography represents a narrow and specialised sub-domain in large-scale medical vision--language pretraining, so the transmitted semantic signal may be weak or misleading. Aligning to a model that has never seen enough coronary images is unlikely to produce representations that are discriminative for vascular anatomy.

JEPA (\textit{Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture}) architectures~\cite{assran2023selfsupervisedlearningimagesjointembedding} offer a self-supervised alternative that removes the dependency on any external model. Rather than reconstructing masked pixels --- which can waste capacity on low-level texture --- JEPA predicts in latent space the representations of masked regions. This forces the encoder to model the contextual relationships between visible patches and occluded areas, learning representations that are inherently structured and high-level. The approach has shown strong results across medical imaging modalities: US-JEPA~\cite{radhachandran2026usjepajointembeddingpredictive} for ultrasound, EchoJEPA~\cite{munim2026echojepalatentpredictivefoundation} for echocardiography, Brain-JEPA~\cite{NEURIPS2024_9c3828ad} for fMRI, and Seg-JEPA~\cite{10.1007/978-981-92-0068-9_7} for segmentation.

We propose replacing stage 2's BioMedCLIP alignment with JEPA training directly on coronary angiography images, with no dependency on an external model. Following MedVAE's two-stage recipe on ARCADE, stage 1 trains an autoencoder of the MedVAE family from scratch with reconstruction, perceptual and adversarial losses (15 epochs on the 1\,200 images of ARCADE's training and validation splits, 1\,080 for training and 120 for model selection; the test images are never seen). This autoencoder is smaller than the released \texttt{medvae\_4\_1\_2d} (64 base channels, four resolution levels, 21M parameters) and compresses by a factor of 8 per side, into a $64\times64\times1$ latent. Stage 2 starts from the stage-1 weights and keeps the decoder frozen throughout. Unlike MedVAE's stage 2, which trains only projection layers, it trains the encoder itself: our experiment therefore tests an \emph{adaptation of the encoder} by JEPA, and the reconstruction can degrade by construction, since the frozen decoder was trained on the stage-1 encoder.

\subsection{Method}

\paragraph{Original stage 2 --- BioMedCLIP alignment.}
In the original MedVAE formulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:stage2ori}), stage 2 fine-tunes the encoder by minimising the distance between the VAE's latent representations and BioMedCLIP embeddings for the same images. This step aims to semantically enrich the latent space by transmitting the knowledge of a pre-trained medical foundation model.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/yanic/stage_2_medvae_ori.png}
    \caption{Original stage 2: latent space alignment on BioMedCLIP embeddings.}
    \label{fig:stage2ori}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Modified stage 2 --- JEPA adaptation.}
We replace the BioMedCLIP alignment with JEPA training directly on coronary angiography images (Fig.~\ref{fig:stage2}). The architecture has three components, all operating on the encoder's penultimate feature maps (256 channels at $64\times64$, just before the projection to the single-channel latent):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Context encoder}: the stage-1 encoder (frozen decoder), receiving the masked image (40\% of patches zeroed) and producing the context features $z_c$.
    \item \textbf{Target encoder}: EMA copy of the context encoder with \texttt{stop-gradient}, encoding the full image. Weights updated as $\theta_t \leftarrow m\,\theta_t + (1-m)\,\theta_c$ ($m = 0{.}996$).
    \item \textbf{JEPA predictor}: lightweight convolutional network (depth 3, hidden width 512) taking $z_c$ concatenated with a binary mask of target patches, predicting $\hat{z}_t$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/yanic/stage_2_pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{Modified stage 2: JEPA adaptation --- the context encoder predicts the target latent of the EMA encoder (stop-grad).}
    \label{fig:stage2}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Loss function.}
The stage-2 loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{stage2}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} + \lambda_{\text{SIGReg}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}},
    \label{eq:stage2}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}$ is the Smooth-$L_1$ loss between $\hat{z}_t$ and the channel-wise normalised target latent $\tilde{z}_t$, restricted to masked patches $\mathcal{M}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{M}} \mathrm{SmoothL_1}\!\left(\hat{z}_t^{(p)},\; \tilde{z}_t^{(p)}\right),
\end{equation}
and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}}$ is the SIGReg regularisation~\cite{balestriero2025lejepaprovablescalableselfsupervised} on $z_c$, based on the Epps--Pulley statistic applied to random projections of the embeddings:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}}\!\left[ T_{\text{EP}}\!\left(\bigl\{ z_c^\top \mathbf{v} \bigr\}\right) \right].
\end{equation}
This term prevents representational collapse without negative pairs. In practice $\lambda_{\text{SIGReg}} = 0{.}01$.

\paragraph{Collapse risks.}
JEPA training is exposed to three degenerate modes. \textbf{Representational collapse} occurs when the encoder produces a constant representation that the predictor learns to imitate --- normalising $\tilde{z}_t$ channel-wise makes this solution explicitly penalised. \textbf{Dimensional collapse} corresponds to the progressive extinction of certain latent dimensions; $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SIGReg}}$ detects and corrects this by maintaining an isotropic distribution over random projections. Finally, a \textbf{too-low EMA momentum} would cause the target encoder to converge too quickly towards the context encoder, cancelling the learning signal --- hence the choice of $m = 0{.}996$, which keeps the target encoder as a slow-moving average.

At the end of stage 2, one expects a latent space where patches that are anatomically similar produce nearby representations regardless of their spatial position. The predictor acts as a local world model: inferring a masked patch requires understanding the global structure of the image, which forces the encoder to acquire semantically informative representations rather than encoding low-level intensity patterns.

\subsection{Results}

\emph{Note on this revised version.} In an earlier version of this study, the ARCADE test images were part of the pre-training data, and the latent-space analysis (effective rank, number of principal components, $k$-NN and linear probe) was computed on the output of the channel-projection head $\texttt{channel\_proj}(\texttt{channel\_ds}(z)+z)$, a layer trained neither by the stage-1 loss nor by the JEPA loss; its conclusion that stage 2 concentrates the variance on 25 dimensions described a random projection of the latent. Both stages were retrained without the test images, and the representation is now evaluated on the layer that JEPA actually trains, on the 300 test images.

\paragraph{Reconstruction quality.}
During stage 2 the decoder remains frozen, so any displacement of the encoder into a subspace incompatible with the decoder directly degrades reconstruction (Table~\ref{tab:jepa}, Fig.~\ref{fig:recon}). Stage 1 reconstructs at $30.2$~dB, below the released MedVAE ($34.8$~dB), which compresses half as much per side and was trained on a million images; stage 2 collapses to $17.8$~dB, the frozen decoder no longer reading the representation of the adapted encoder.

\paragraph{What the encoder has learnt.}
We measure how linearly the trained features separate vessels from background: a linear probe (per-channel standardisation, $1\times1$ convolution, bilinear upsampling to $512\times512$) is trained on the 1\,000 training images, selected on the 200 validation images and evaluated on the test set, with three seeds. The probe must be trained to convergence: with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and 20 epochs, all scores were still rising at the last epoch; we report the converged setting (learning rate $10^{-2}$, 100 epochs), whose ranking is the same as with 20 and 60 epochs. A probe predicting the 25 artery segments, or using the single-channel latent, reaches a Dice of about $0.03$ for every model: a per-pixel linear classifier can neither name a segment nor separate vessels from a single channel. We also measure the dimensionality of the trained features (effective rank of the per-pixel feature vectors and number of principal components explaining $90\,\%$ of their variance).

\section{MedVAE as a Latent Representation for Coronary Segmentation}
\label{sec:segmentation}

The high resolution of medical images is necessary to preserve diagnostic details, but it strongly increases the computational and storage cost of deep learning models. An attractive solution consists of compressing images using generic large-scale pre-trained variational autoencoders (VAE) \cite{kingma2014vae}, of which MedVAE \cite{varma2025medvaeefficientautomatedinterpretation} is a representative example.

This compression remains however intrinsically destructive, and nothing guarantees that it preserves information useful for all downstream tasks. The benefits of MedVAE have been established primarily on classification and detection tasks, which rely on global features. Fine coronary artery segmentation constitutes on the contrary an unfavourable case. Indeed, the structures to be delineated are thin, tubular, and low-contrast, the number of classes is high (26 classes: background and 25 arterial segments), and their distribution is heavily imbalanced. The central question we pose is therefore: \textbf{does lossy medical compression preserve sufficient anatomical information to enable multi-class coronary segmentation? A second methodological question arises alongside: at which point in the processing pipeline must the compression be integrated for this information to remain exploitable?}

To answer this, we evaluate on the public ARCADE dataset \cite{popov2024arcade} the extent to which MedVAE can serve as an intermediate representation for segmentation. We compare three strategies accounting for compression against a full-resolution baseline, namely a U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} trained on original images: (i) segmenting directly from the compressed latent representation with a dedicated segmentation head, (ii) first adapting the compressor to the coronary domain by fine-tuning before segmenting from the latent, and (iii) training a segmentation model on the decompressed images. These three strategies cover both latent-space and reconstructed pixel-space usage, with and without domain adaptation. Our results show that the compression preserves the information needed for segmentation in both spaces: segmenting the decompressed images is as accurate as segmenting the originals, and segmenting directly from the single-channel latent reaches more than $90\,\%$ of the baseline's Dice.

\emph{Note on this revised version.} In an earlier version of this study, segmentation from the latent collapsed to predicting the background everywhere (Dice $0.04$), which led us to conclude that the latent was unfit for dense prediction. This collapse came from the training, not from the latent: the head was trained from scratch with a learning rate of $5\times10^{-5}$, too low to leave the trivial solution, and it had no convolution at the latent resolution, hence almost no spatial context. The earlier version also reported IoU values computed incorrectly (index masks passed to a metric expecting one-hot masks, which yielded IoU values above the Dice), selected models on 20\,\% of the training images instead of the official validation split, used a Gaussian-noise augmentation that the current version of Albumentations makes more than ten times stronger than intended, and relied on a single training run per condition. All results below were obtained after correcting these points, with three training seeds per condition.

\subsection{Methods}

\paragraph{Data and Preprocessing.}
We use the segmentation subset of ARCADE \cite{popov2024arcade} ($512 \times 512$ greyscale angiographies), whose COCO annotations are converted into dense 26-class masks (background and 25 arterial segments). Models are trained on the 1\,000 training images, selected on the 200 images of the official validation split, and evaluated on the official test set of 300 images. Intensities are normalised in $[0,1]$ and training employs standard augmentation (flips, affine transforms, contrast and brightness adjustments, light Gaussian noise of standard deviation $3$ to $7$ grey levels, CLAHE) via Albumentations \cite{buslaev2020albumentations}. Geometric transforms are applied jointly to the image and its mask to preserve alignment.

\paragraph{MedVAE Compressor.}
The compressor is the \texttt{medvae\_4\_1\_2d} variant of MedVAE \cite{varma2025medvaeefficientautomatedinterpretation}, which reduces each spatial dimension by a factor of 4: a $512 \times 512$ image is encoded into a single-channel $128 \times 128$ latent, a factor-16 reduction in the number of values. Unless stated otherwise, its weights are frozen. Conditions B and C use the stage-1 latent (the mean-and-variance bottleneck used for reconstruction), not the output of MedVAE's stage-2 projection layers, which MedVAE intends for downstream tasks.

\paragraph{Experimental Conditions.}
We compare three strategies accounting for compression against a full-resolution baseline, as well as a diagnostic control (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}).

\textbf{A (baseline).} A U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} with a ResNet-34 \cite{he2016resnet} encoder pre-trained on ImageNet (24.4M parameters), trained end-to-end on original images.

\textbf{A$^{*}$ (compression alone).} The U-Net from condition A, without any retraining, is applied to images first encoded then decoded by MedVAE. This condition isolates the raw effect of pixel-space compression on an unadapted model, independently of any learning.

\textbf{B (segmentation from the latent).} The $128 \times 128 \times 1$ latent produced by the frozen MedVAE encoder feeds a segmentation head trained from scratch: a small U-Net operating at the latent resolution (three downsampling levels, $128 \to 64 \to 32 \to 16$, with skip connections and 64 base channels), followed by two $\times 2$ upsampling blocks back to $512 \times 512$ and a $1 \times 1$ classifier into 26 classes (7.8M parameters). The convolutions at the latent resolution give the head the spatial context needed to identify a segment, which depends on its position in the coronary tree.

\textbf{C (domain fine-tuning).} Identical to B, but the MedVAE autoencoder is first fine-tuned on ARCADE with a reconstruction loss before being frozen, in order to test whether domain adaptation enriches the latent.

\textbf{D (segmentation of reconstructed images).} A U-Net identical to A is trained not on original images but on their MedVAE-reconstructed versions. Unlike A$^{*}$, the network here adapts to the compression artefacts.

\label{fig:conditions}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Training and Evaluation.}
All models minimise a composite loss combining Dice loss and cross-entropy in equal parts:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = 0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}} + 0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}.
\end{equation}
This choice directly addresses the massive class imbalance. The Dice loss \cite{milletari2016vnet} measures the overlap between prediction and ground truth independently of region size, preventing the dominant background from controlling optimisation. Cross-entropy, computed pixel-wise, provides a more stable gradient, especially at the start of training. Optimisation uses AdamW with cosine learning-rate scheduling (initial learning rate $10^{-4}$ for the U-Nets, $10^{-3}$ for the heads of B and C, which are trained from scratch), mixed precision, at most 100 epochs and early stopping on the validation Dice. Each condition is trained with three seeds; A$^{*}$ uses the seed-42 model of A. Dice and IoU are computed per class over the whole test set and averaged over the artery segments present in the ground truth or in the prediction (background excluded), which we call the \emph{artery Dice} and \emph{artery IoU}.

\paragraph{Segmentation from the latent works.}
With an adequate head and learning rate, conditions B and C reach an artery Dice of $0.393$ and $0.403$, that is $91\,\%$ and $94\,\%$ of the baseline A ($0.430$), from a representation sixteen times smaller than the image. They detect the same segments as A (Fig.~\ref{fig:seg_results}), with the largest gaps on thin and rare branches, and their predictions follow the coronary tree (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}). Fine-tuning the compressor on ARCADE helps slightly: C beats or equals B for the three seeds ($+0.010$ on average), a gap of the order of the seed-to-seed variability.

\paragraph{Pixel-space compression is nearly neutral.}
Condition A$^{*}$ measures the effect of compression alone: applying the baseline model, without retraining, to MedVAE-reconstructed images costs only $0.006$ of artery Dice ($0.425$ against $0.431$ for the same seed), and its predictions are nearly identical to those of A (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}). The $16\times$ compression therefore preserves the anatomical information exploited by the network when rendered in pixel space.

\paragraph{Adapting to reconstruction brings no measurable gain.}
Condition D reaches an artery Dice of $0.432 \pm 0.004$, against $0.430 \pm 0.005$ for A: the difference ($+0.003$ of Dice, with one of the three seeds in favour of A) is within the seed-to-seed variability, and the per-class profiles of A and D are nearly identical. The earlier version of this study reported a gain of D over A concentrated on thin segments; with several seeds and the corrected augmentation this gain disappears. A plausible explanation is the overly strong noise augmentation of the earlier version, which degraded about 30\,\% of A's training images while D received them after MedVAE, which removes such noise (Section~\ref{sec:medvae-robustness}).

\paragraph{Segmentation of degraded images.}
Section~\ref{sec:medvae-robustness} showed that MedVAE removes Poisson noise; does this make segmentation more robust? We degraded the 300 test images with the same Poisson noise, JPEG compression and blur (levels 0, 3, 6 and 9 of the ten-level grid of Section~\ref{sec:medvae-robustness}) and segmented them with A, A$^{*}$ and D (three seeds, Fig.~\ref{fig:seg_robustness}). MedVAE's denoising does not help: under Poisson noise, A$^{*}$ is \emph{worse} than A at weak and medium levels ($-0.02$ to $-0.04$), the network trained on clean images being more disturbed by the reconstructions of noisy images than by the noise itself, and D behaves like A. All three models collapse under the strongest degradations (artery Dice below $0.12$). Robustness depends instead on what the network sees during training: the overly strong noise augmentation of the earlier version made A far more robust (Dice $0.34$ under the strongest Poisson noise, against $0.03$ with the corrected augmentation), and adding realistic degradations to the training augmentation (Poisson noise, JPEG, blur, applied to 30\,\% of the images) restores most of it ($0.27$ under the strongest Poisson noise, $0.29$ under the strongest JPEG compression, $0.37$ under the strongest blur) for a cost on clean images within the seed-to-seed variability ($0.423 \pm 0.011$ against $0.430 \pm 0.005$). Since these degradations are the same families as those evaluated, this last result measures in-distribution robustness.

\subsection{Discussion}

Our experiments address both aspects of the problem. Regarding information preservation, a factor-16 lossy medical compression retains sufficient anatomical information for multi-class coronary segmentation: in pixel space, compression alone costs almost nothing (A$^{*}$ $\approx$ A) and a model trained on reconstructions matches the baseline (D $\approx$ A). Regarding the integration point, MedVAE's single-channel latent is directly exploitable for dense prediction, provided the head has convolutions at the latent resolution and is trained with an adequate learning rate: it then reaches more than $90\,\%$ of the baseline's Dice, with a head three times smaller than the baseline U-Net and inputs sixteen times smaller.

A gap of about $0.03$ of Dice nevertheless remains between the latent and the image, concentrated on thin and rare segments. A multi-channel latent, or exploiting intermediate encoder feature maps rather than the sole bottleneck, could reduce it; likewise, fine-tuning the encoder with a segmentation objective rather than a reconstruction one is a natural direction. Our results thus delineate the regime in which a generic medical compressor is useful for segmentation: as a pixel-space preprocessor, it is harmless, and as a latent representation, it is a compact and nearly sufficient input for dense prediction.

\section{Perspectives}

The experiments presented in this paper suggest a natural research direction: the construction of a \emph{foundation model for radiation-based medical imaging}, unifying the two complementary axes explored here --- semantic structuring of the latent space via JEPA and understanding of acquisition-specific degradations.

\paragraph{A hybrid JEPA--reconstruction objective.}
With a frozen decoder, the JEPA adaptation of Section~\ref{sec:jepa} degrades both the reconstruction and the linear separability of vessels. A patch-prediction objective may nonetheless become useful for inducing semantically structured representations in medical imaging, provided it is combined with a reconstruction branch from the outset. Such a hybrid objective would simultaneously predict masked latent patches and reconstruct the input image, keeping the encoder anchored to a space compatible with the decoder. These two objectives are complementary --- the first pushes the model to capture high-level contextual relationships between patches, while the second prevents the latent from drifting into a subspace that is no longer decodable. Balancing them via a learnable weight or an annealing schedule would allow progressive control of the semantic/reconstruction trade-off throughout training.

\paragraph{Degradations modelled for radiation-based imaging.}
The robustness analysis of Section~\ref{sec:medvae-robustness} reveals that the nature of the degradation, and not merely its intensity, determines the model's behaviour. A foundation model should therefore be pre-trained on a degradation distribution that faithfully reflects clinical acquisition conditions. For X-ray and fluoroscopy, we envision a composite forward model grounded in the physical image formation process, combining several sources of variability. Quantum shot noise, dominant at low dose, would be simulated by controlling the photon flux. Luminosity and contrast variations, caused by changes in exposure or detector response, would be modelled by a global brightness factor and a non-linear contrast transform applied to the grey-level dynamic range, both of which have direct physical interpretations in terms of dose and detector gain. Spatially smooth multiplicative and additive fields would account for gain non-uniformities, vignetting, and scattered radiation inherent to X-ray beam heterogeneity. Finally, anisotropic motion blur kernels with orientations drawn from distributions fitted to cardiac and respiratory dynamics would extend the isotropic blur studied here to clinically realistic directional blurring. Sampling these parameters independently and composing the resulting transforms would yield a rich, physically grounded augmentation space, exposing the model to the full spectrum of artefacts that currently cause reconstruction to collapse, and allowing the JEPA objective to learn representations that are robust to the acquisition variability inherent in radiation-based imaging.

\paragraph{Conditional training via a CVAE.}
Beyond robustness, an important axis consists of making the model \emph{modality-aware} and \emph{quality-aware}. Section~\ref{sec:theophile} shows that a quality score computed from the image itself brings no information to an autoencoder of that image; conditioning is only promising with signals that are not a function of the image. The latent space could thus be structured by conditioning the encoder on metadata available in DICOM headers: acquisition modality (fluoroscopy, CT, MRI), patient dose, tube current, or reconstruction parameters. The natural framework for this is a \emph{conditional VAE} (CVAE)~\cite{sohn2015cvae}: the conditioning signal, concatenated or injected via FiLM layers~\cite{perez2018film}, would guide the encoder to project the image into a sub-region of the latent space corresponding to the acquisition context. Such a model would learn disentangled representations where the anatomical content is separated from the acquisition quality, enabling downstream tasks such as cross-modality transfer, quality-aware retrieval, or targeted denoising adapted to the estimated degradation level. The modality and quality metadata, always available in clinical practice, could thus be converted from a source of latent variability into a structuring prior for training.

\paragraph{Downstream validation.}
Ultimately, the value of such a foundation model can only be established by evaluating the quality of its representations on concrete downstream tasks. We would aim to validate it across a range of benchmarks spanning classification, detection, and segmentation tasks over multiple modalities. Coronary artery segmentation on the ARCADE dataset constitutes a particularly challenging and clinically relevant test bed: as shown in this paper, it requires fine spatial resolution, tolerance to acquisition artefacts, and discriminative power over a large number of anatomically thin and imbalanced classes --- precisely the properties that the proposed training objectives are designed to foster.

\section{Conclusion}

Across four complementary axes, we probed how a generic large-scale medical compressor, MedVAE, behaves on the demanding domain of coronary angiography. Measuring restoration against the clean image, our robustness analysis showed that MedVAE reduces Poisson noise, though 4 to 5~dB less than a simple $5\times5$ filter, and restores neither JPEG artefacts nor blur; this noise reduction does not make the segmentation of degraded images more robust. Conditioning the autoencoder on an image-quality score through FiLM, trained and evaluated rigorously, neither improves nor degrades its reconstruction, and the network barely uses the score, which is a function of the image it already sees; fine-tuning MedVAE on ARCADE, in contrast, gains more than 7~dB. Replacing MedVAE's stage-2 BioMedCLIP alignment with a self-supervised JEPA objective, with a frozen decoder, degrades both the reconstruction and the linear separability of vessels relative to the stage-1 autoencoder it starts from, without the dimensional contraction reported earlier. Finally, a $16\times$ lossy compression preserves the anatomical information needed for fine multi-class coronary segmentation: segmenting reconstructed images matches the original ones, and the single-channel latent, with a head that has convolutions at its resolution, reaches more than $90\,\%$ of the full-image Dice.

Taken together, our results sketch a coherent picture: MedVAE's compression is anatomically faithful, reusable in pixel space and, to a large extent, directly in its latent space; the attempts to make it acquisition-aware or semantically richer did not help in the forms we tested. Several conclusions of the first version of this study were reversed once measurement and training issues were corrected, which underlines how much such conclusions depend on the protocol: a metric comparing to the right reference, identical training for the compared models, several seeds, and an evaluation on the layer that is actually trained. The way forward we advocate, detailed in our perspectives, is to unify these axes into a single foundation model for radiation-based medical imaging, coupling a hybrid JEPA--reconstruction objective with a physically grounded model of clinical degradations and explicit conditioning on acquisition metadata.

\section{Vessel-Masked PSNR: Details}
\label{app:mpsnr}

The masked PSNR ($\mathrm{mPSNR}$) restricts the error computation to vessel pixels only, in order to eliminate the bias of the background, which is largely dominant and clinically irrelevant. ARCADE's polygonal annotations are rasterised into a binary mask (\texttt{cv2.fillPoly}), then resized by nearest-neighbour interpolation to preserve their binary nature. The mean squared error is evaluated only over this set of pixels, denoted $\mathcal{M}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{mPSNR}(x, y) = 10\,\log_{10}\!\frac{D^2}
{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{p \in \mathcal{M}} \big(x_p - y_p\big)^2},
\label{eq:mpsnr}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the intensity range ($D = 1$ for images normalised in $[0,1]$).
